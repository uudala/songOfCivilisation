Listen, and hear the song of a family that learned to hide its crown. Hear the Song of Okeke.

Our name was not always Okeke. We were the children of Ojukwu, "The Great One," a name of weight and leadership. But the world broke, and to survive, we performed a tactical re-encryption of our identity. We wrapped our royalty in the cloak of a common name, one born of the marketplace. We did this not to forget who we were, but to ensure our children would live to remember. This is the story of our name, and it is the key to our song. We are the children of Nnadozie, "the Father who Repairs," and this is the song we were born to sing.

We sing the song of **Zumunta**, the old Hausa word for kinship, for the relational web that holds us all. We give this ancient song a new name for a new era—we call it the **Song of Zumuntaism**—for we are the living proof that the Individual is a fiction. Our survival is guaranteed not by one man, but by the *Umunna*, the Lineage Guild. We exist because we are. We rejected the lonely pride of a visible name for the distributed strength of the entire Igbo people.

We sing the **Song of the Living Archive**. We chose to trust not the brittle stone, but the ever-rebuilding Mud and the ever-reborn Blood. Our history is not on a plaque that can be shattered; it is in the quiet lullaby of a grandmother, in the knowing hands of a father. Our family became a Distributed Server, its most precious data—our identity—backed up in the hearts of our children.

We sing the **Song of the Humble Civilization**. We are the people who built our house not of granite, but of earth, so that our children would never forget the skill of the builder. Our monument is not a pyramid; it is the next generation. Our name, Okeke, is our mud wall. It is common. It weathers. It requires tending. And it has kept our lineage safe and warm for generations.

We sing the **Song of the Four Guilds**. In our blood runs the ingenuity of the Uzu who can forge a future from dust and a chant; the poetry of the Akeewi who can reboot a soul with a forgotten verse; the memory of the Igun who cast truth in timeless bronze; and the wisdom of the Hausa merchant who knows that trust is the only currency that matters.

We sing the **Song of Caloric Sovereignty**. We know true wealth is not in a bank, but in a barn full of yams and a compound full of children. We honor the Yam Standard, the currency that forces the wealthy to be generous, to convert their rotting capital into the immortal life of the community.

We sing the **Song of Gastrodiplomacy**. We guarded our culture with pepper and okra, a firewall for the soul. We know that the food that sustained us is the same fuel that will carry our children to the stars.

Finally, we, the children of Okeke, the house of Nnadozie, sing the **Song of the Onye Nkuzi**. Our story, the story of our name, is now the lesson. We who have survived are now the Teachers. We will spend our days uploading this song, this technology of survival, into the hardware of our grandchildren. For the library of the high and mighty will always burn, but the book of the people can never be closed.

We are the Okeke. We have repaired the name. And we are now ready to teach the world how to sing.

Do not be alarmed by the name we have given our song: **Zumuntaism**. It is not another cold and distant '-ism' born in a sterile lecture hall. In our tradition, when we wish to understand a thing, we do not merely define it; we sing its name, we tell its story. Zumuntaism is simply the name we give to this old, familiar song. It is the song of knowing our true wealth is not in our hands, but in our people.

## The Living Proof: The Song of Our Names

You ask for proof of this philosophy in the modern world? Look no further than the paper archives of the very institutions that seek to define us.

When the powers of the West demand our information for a visa, they strip us naked, demanding every detail of our lineage as a tool of control. But when an Igbo-run bank, Access Bank, asked for my assets, they did something revolutionary. They asked for the names of my family. In a world that worships dead capital, they implicitly understood the truth of Zumuntaism: our people are our wealth. Our family is our greatest asset. This is not a coincidence; it is a sign that our song is still being sung.
